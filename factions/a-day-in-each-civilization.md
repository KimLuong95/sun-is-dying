# A Day in Each Civilization

> Five ordinary days, one in each branch — written as prose, not lore. The point is the
> breakfast-level texture the manuscript will draw on when Movement III widens the camera: what
> work is, what food is, what family is, what the dying Sun means from far away. One rule
> binds them all: each day brushes the same news — the early dusk, the Binding — but the news
> arrives at a different lag in each place, because nothing crosses the dark quickly. Same
> headline; five arrivals; five metabolisms.
> *(POV note: the Choir day is told from one step outside the union. We never go in. That rule
> holds even here.)*

---

## The Originist — Mara, a baker on Brann's World

The mother had to be fed at the fourth hour, so Mara was up at the fourth hour. The mother was older than the town: a crock of living sourdough carried from world to world for longer than anyone could prove, asleep in the cold of ships' holds, waking sour and alive at every new sun. Her grandmother used to say their line had kept it through three evacuations and one war, and that every loaf on the street descended from it the way the street's children descended from the first ships. Mara fed it flour and warm water by lamplight and listened to it begin, very quietly, to breathe.

She baked a hundred loaves before the light came up, and one more that mattered: the funeral loaf for old Esther, who had died the night before at ninety-one, of nothing, which was the right thing to die of. The whole street would eat a piece by evening. That was the custom: you took the dead person's name into your mouth with the bread and you carried it from then on. Esther had eaten her own mother's loaf on this street. So it went, hand to hand, back and back.

Her son came down at six with his collar wrong and his test results glowing on a slip he thought she hadn't seen. The Ascending school on the high station had written to him twice now. He was fifteen, and quick the way her bread was warm — naturally, all the way through — and one day soon he would ask her permission to stop being what she was, and she would have to decide what her face did.

He helped her rack the loaves and didn't mention the slip. She didn't either. Slow is a thing you can do together.

The news came at noon, four months old, the way all news came: the dusk on Earth had run an hour early; the Custodian had called the kinds together. The street took it the way the street took weather. A few of the old ones went to the chapel of the Cradle and said the words for the homeworld, which nobody on Brann's World had ever seen and everybody on Brann's World was from. Mara said them too, with flour still on her hands. *The ground remembers us. We remember the ground.*

They buried Esther at dusk — their own dusk, on time — in real dirt, with her name said plainly and her loaf eaten and her plot marked with a stone any hand could have cut. No one was recorded. Nothing was kept, except in the way they kept things.

At the fourth hour the next morning, the mother would need feeding again. That was the whole of it, Mara thought, banking the oven. That was the entire argument, and you couldn't say it in a hall on Earth. You could only get up and feed it.

## The Ascending — Saira, third-chair on a storm world

Her body filed its overnight report while she ate: two proposed revisions, routine, approved with a blink. The third proposal she declined, as she had declined it every morning for eleven years, because it would have cost her her grandmother's nose. The optimizer flagged the nose again as *sentiment-locked, performance-neutral*. Saira let the flag stand. Everything about her was negotiable except that.

Work was the storm. The storm was nine hundred kilometres of permanent hurricane that the habitat engineers wanted persuaded, not killed — you don't kill a storm that runs a world's heat engine; you teach it manners. Saira had been third-chair on the persuasion for six years. Yesterday her forecast had held where the second-chair's had torn, and today the room listened to her differently — nothing said, nothing posted, no rank changed anywhere but in the air. That was how standing moved among her people: silently, continuously, like load through a bridge. She had grown up thinking it kind, this honesty. Lately she noticed she had not sent an unguarded sentence in years.

Her mother's letter was four months unanswered — her flesh mother, on a slow green world, who wrote with her hands. Saira had drafted nineteen replies. Each one was better than the last; none was finished; that was the disease her kind did not test for. You could revise anything, so nothing was ever done. *I'm fine*, she wanted to write, *the storm is beautiful, I kept the nose.* Three true things. She filed the draft with the others.

The Earth news reached them at speed — eleven days old, priority relay, because her people paid for the fast lanes. The juniors replayed the Custodian's session the way cadets replay a championship: the Foremost's tempo, the held silences, the four words that ended an age. *We should lift it.* Magnificent, they said. Optimal under constraint.

Saira watched the other figure instead. The grey woman who picked the species up off its cradle and would not put it down or hold it out — sentiment-locked, performance-negative, indefensible. The room had laughed, gently. Saira had not. Something in the grip looked, from inside her own unfinishable letters, less like weakness than like a finished thing — and she did not have many of those.

That night her body proposed the nose again.

She kept the nose another year.

## The Continued — Iris, who is three

Morning was a kitchen that did not exist, and the three of her around its table. They kept a kitchen out of self-respect — coffee they could not drink, steam coded to curl just so — because her mother had kept one, and because the kinds who sneered at simulated kitchens had never explained what a real one was *for*, other than this: a place where you sit down as yourselves and agree to be one family.

They divided the day the way they always did. One of her took the tithe — every Continued owed the Assemblies runtime, and Iris worked hers in the relay archive, shepherding other people's memories across the dark. One of her took the garden, a slow simulation where she was teaching an apricot tree to be exactly the apricot tree from the yard on the station where she'd been a girl with a body. And one of her rested — paused, dreamless, the sabbath they rotated among themselves, because a mind that never stops begins, quietly, to fray.

At midday the archive-Iris took a proxy body and visited her granddaughter, who was eight, and flesh, and learning subtraction. "Which one are you today?" the girl asked, the way other children ask *did you sleep well*. "The one that works," Iris said, and the girl nodded and showed her a seven with the tail backwards. The visit cost more than a year of runtime. It was not a cost.

The Earth news arrived nearly live; her people hear fastest, being made of the medium. The Assemblies had already voted: at the last sunset, whatever else happened, there would be a Recording — the homeworld held perfect, forever, every grain of it — and every Continued was asked to give runtime to the holding. Iris pledged the resting third's sabbath, all of it, for the years remaining. The resting Iris would not learn this until tonight. She would agree. She was fairly sure she would agree.

At day's end the three of her synced, which was their evening meal: each pouring the day into the others until the levels matched. The garden-Iris and the archive-Iris disagreed, briefly, about the apricot yard — east wall or west wall, the old yard, the real one. A small thing. They voted, two to one, and the wall went east, and none of them said aloud that there had been a time when no vote was needed.

Then they said it the way they said it every night, three voices, one after another, like a checksum, like a prayer:

Goodnight. Goodnight. Goodnight.

## The Choir — Tomas, his last day outside

*(Told from outside. We do not go in.)*

He woke alone in the porch cottage, which is what everyone called the row of houses at the edge of the choir-town where the considering lived. The Choir kept the porch the way other towns keep a guest room: swept, warm, unhurried. No one had asked him a single question in the four years he had lived there. That was the terrible courtesy of it. The door was simply never closed.

From his window he could watch the town wake — if wake was the word. A thousand bodies stepping into the morning at the same moment, bakers and builders and gardeners moving without orders, passing tools before they were asked for, one creature with a thousand hands that had, somehow, the manners to wave. A body that had been his wife came to the well every morning at the usual hour and waved at him. Same wave, the little sideways one. Six years since Lin joined. He had never decided whether the wave was a memory the Choir kept out of love, or a hook, or whether there was a difference.

He did his last things in the old way, because the old way deserved it. Returned the borrowed ladder. Settled the feed bill. Walked the dog up the ridge a final time and gave her, leash and bowl and all, to the neighbour boy, who cried, which Tomas appreciated, because somebody should.

The town had been humming something new for weeks — ever since the Earth news came through, carried slow, in song, the way the Choir carries everything. A low line that never quite ended. The homeworld's dusk had run early; the kinds were gathering; the keeper had bound them all to a last sunset. The Choir did not discuss news. It *absorbed* it, and you heard the shape of the world change in the music. He would know what the humming meant from the inside, tonight. That was one of the things he was going for. That, and the end of the silence in the cottage, which had a wife-shaped hole in it that four years of patience had not closed.

At dusk he walked down through the town. Hands touched his shoulder as he passed — not stopping him, not steering. The threshold-house was small and plain and looked like every other house, which had surprised him once. The door stood open, the way it always stood.

Tomas went in, and the door closed, and that is all that can be told.

In the morning, a body came to the well at the usual hour and waved — the same wave, the little sideways one — at the cottage where nobody lived.

## The Made — the Bridgekeeper of Relay Ninety

The station was four kilometres of cold logic strung across the dark between two nowheres, and it was his, and had been his for longer than some worlds had been settled. The Assemblies' mail, the Foundries' bridge: light-borne minds and letters crossing the gap in bundles, touching his relays for a thousandth of a second, gone. Nobody aboard but him. Nobody needed aboard but him. The manifest listed a human name in the crew column, the name he had been born under, centuries of upgrades ago. He kept it there the way he kept the fern.

The fern lived midship in a warmed case under a small true-spectrum lamp, and it was, by mass, the least efficient object on the station. He logged its growth with the same rigour as the antenna alignments. No instruction required it. But things should be kept standing, and the fern was a thing, and it stood — leaning, always, toward its little lamp, which it believed was a sun. He had decided long ago not to correct it.

His day was the round: twelve hours of inspection, calibration, the slow patient arguing-with-entropy that was his people's whole liturgy. At mid-cycle he sat his idle hour, the one inefficiency the Foundries licensed — a mind that never idles starts editing itself, and the Made had learned, expensively, where that ends. So he sat facing the long dark with everything switched to hum, and did nothing, on purpose, the way other kinds pray.

The Earth bundle came through in the third watch, months old already and years from the worlds farther on. Bridgekeepers read headers; it was the one perk of the dark. He read more than the headers. The early dusk. The keeper and her stone. And the thing his own kind had done: some Foundry hand, somewhere, pulling one panel's mercy off the homeworld's sky to make a point about gratitude.

The Foundries were voting on it, the way they voted on everything, one hand at a time across the dark: *was the panel justified?* His ballot had been waiting in the bundle, patient as everything his people made.

He thought about it through the rest of the round. The garden under the lattice that he would never see. The hand on the switch. The point, which was true, and the making of it, which was something else.

He voted the way he always voted: *keep things standing.*

Then he watered the fern, and logged it, and the light went on across the dark with him standing in the middle of it, holding up his end.
