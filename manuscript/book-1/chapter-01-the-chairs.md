# Chapter One — The Chairs

*Book 1 · first draft.*

---

Yara carried the last chair in from the cold room and set it at the head of the table. Then she stood back and counted, the way she counted most evenings now, as if the number might come out different.

Five places. She had laid five.

The table could seat twelve. It had, once, when the house was loud and the world past the windows was green and no one had to think very hard about what any of it was for. Now she lived here with an old man and a planet, and she laid five places at a table built for twelve, and even five was a lie she hadn't decided how to tell.

She had laid a plate for Sela.

That was the one she kept coming back to. A plate, a glass, and the small bone-handled fork her daughter had used as a girl. Sela had screamed for that fork once, when it went missing for a week, and Yara had found it behind the stove, and Sela had held it like it was the fork that made food taste of anything at all.

Sela did not eat now.

So the plate was a kindness, or it was the cruelest thing in the room, and Yara had stood over it long enough that the answer should have come. It hadn't.

"You used to lay seven," Elias said behind her.

He was in the doorway, in the chair that carried him, a blanket over his knees though the house was warm. He didn't feel warm and cold the way other people did anymore. He felt them as facts, reported from somewhere a long way off. But he liked the blanket. He said it reminded him of a dog.

"There were seven of us," Yara said.

"There were nine, before you. You wouldn't remember the good chairs." The lower half of his face moved, which was how he smiled now. He wheeled himself closer and looked at the table, at the five places, at the fork, and he said nothing about the fork. That was a mercy, and it cost him something, because Elias loved to name things. Naming them was most of what he had left.

The door at the far end of the hall opened without a knock. Ren had never once knocked, in any of his lives.

"It smells," he called, coming in, "like actual food. Mother. You cooked. With your hands." He bent and kissed the top of her head. He could do that now. He hadn't been taller than her when he left. "You shouldn't have. I mean that almost medically. I can't do much with most of it."

"You can sit at a table," Yara said. "You can hold a fork. The rest is between you and your stomach."

"I don't strictly carry one of those. Not the old model." He was already moving through the room, touching things — the back of a chair, the rim of a candlestick — the way he'd touched everything as a boy to prove it was his. "It's fine. I'll perform the meal. I'm very convincing. I've had practice."

"Sit, then."

"In a moment." He'd reached the window, and he stopped there, looking out at the long gold of the evening over the garden, and for a second the cleverness went out of his face and left something younger under it. Then he turned around and it was back. "It's strange, being home," he said, to no one. "Everything's so small, and it all still fits."

And then Sela came in, and the room did the small thing it always did, where everyone arranged their faces before they knew they were doing it.

She wore one of the plain bodies for it. Yara was grateful, and hated being grateful. The body was the colour of nothing much, smooth at the joints, a little too even when it walked, like someone doing a careful and loving impression of a person. But the face was close. They always got the face close. It was Sela's face from the year before she went. Twenty years old, caught and kept.

"You laid me a place," Sela said.

"I laid everyone a place."

"You laid me the fork." Her voice was warm. It was always warm now, and patient — the patience of someone explaining colour to a room that has decided she's blind. She crossed and put both smooth hands on the back of the chair and did not sit. "That's new."

"Sit down," Yara said, to the salt, to the candles, to the middle distance. "It's getting cold."

They sat. The four of them, and the empty places, and the candles Yara had lit because her mother lit candles, and her mother's mother, back and back along the line to people who had lit them against a real dark.

"Is he coming?" Ren asked. He meant his father. He didn't say his father. It turned out there were several names this table had agreed, without ever agreeing, not to say.

"Tomorrow," Yara said. "For the. Tomorrow. Not for this."

"For the talking," Ren said.

"For the talking."

Tomorrow they would sit in a larger room, with people who were not family, and decide what to do about the world. Yara had kept that world her whole life. Her mother had kept it before her. Tomorrow strangers would help her decide whether to save it, and how, and what the word *save* even meant when no two people in the room meant the same thing by it. She had stopped being able to hold tomorrow in her head without her hands going cold. So she thought about the soup instead, and passed it, and watched her family pretend.

Elias ate slowly and with enormous attention, the way he did everything, as if each spoonful were a country he was passing through and might not pass again. "It's good," he told her. "It tastes like." And then he stopped, and his eyes went somewhere she couldn't follow, and he didn't finish — because the thing it tasted like was gone, and had been gone for longer than there had been words for soup, and he was the only one at the table who had ever had it. Even he could only almost remember.

Sela pointed the proxy's eyes at her mother. The body didn't need to look anywhere to see. She did it because it was a thing you did with a face, for a mother.

"You're thinner," Sela said.

"I'm old."

"You're not old. Not for one of you." A pause, very small. "You should let me look at you. Properly, from the inside. I could tell you things your doctors can't."

"Eat your dinner," Yara said.

She heard it as it left her. So did Sela. So did everyone at the table. The candles burned, and no one said the obvious thing, which was that there was nothing on Sela's plate she could ever eat, and that her mother had just told her to eat it anyway.

It was Elias who noticed first.

He had set down his spoon and was looking past Yara to the window, where the evening came in across the table the way it came every night at this hour — gold thinning to amber, amber going down into the deep, familiar red that meant the day was finished.

Except the red was coming too fast.

Yara turned. The light was running down the garden wall like something spilled. The shadow of the dead apple tree reached across the grass and kept reaching. The gold was already gone. The amber went while she watched. The red came on the way blood comes up through a bandage, an hour early, a whole hour, and the air in the room turned the colour of the inside of a mouth.

No one moved.

Then Yara's hand went out across the table. It was the old reflex, the one that had reached for her children in the dark for thirty years, before they became things the dark couldn't reach. Her hand went toward Sela.

It stopped halfway. She still did not know if there was anyone in there to hold.

Elias looked at the red light coming through the glass the way you would look at a face you had not seen in a very long time.

"I didn't think I'd live to see another one," he said.

He sounded almost glad.
