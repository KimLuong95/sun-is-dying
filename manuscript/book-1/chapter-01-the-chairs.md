# Chapter One — The Chairs

*Book 1 · draft 4.*

---

Yara carried the last chair in from the cold room and set it at the head of the table. Then she counted the places. She counted them most evenings now, as if the number might come out different.

Five. She had laid five.

The table sat twelve. It had been full once, when the house was loud and the world outside was green and nobody asked what any of it was for. Now it was her and an old man and a planet, and she laid five places at a table built for twelve, and even five was more than she could be sure of.

She had laid a plate for Sela.

That was the one she kept stopping on. A plate, a glass, and the small bone-handled fork Sela had used as a girl. She'd screamed for that fork once when it went missing, a whole week of screaming, and Yara had found it behind the stove. Sela had held it like it was the fork and not the food that tasted of anything.

Sela didn't eat now.

So the plate was a kindness, or it was a cruelty, and Yara had stood over it long enough to decide, and she hadn't.

And she had laid only the one place for her. There were several of Sela now, last anyone had told her — running in rooms Yara didn't ask about, thinking thoughts she would never hear, all of them as much her daughter as the one that would walk through the door tonight. She had laid one plate. She didn't know if that was a hope or an insult, and there was no one left alive she could have asked.

She went to the window because she couldn't keep standing over the fork.

Outside, the garden ran down to the wall in the last of the light, and past the wall the sky was crowded. They had been arriving for a month. Ships, and things that had stopped bothering to look like ships, holding still in their thousands above a world that had not held a crowd in longer than she could stand to think about. Tomorrow all of it would come down into one room and decide whether Earth went on. Tonight it was only lights, packed close and patient, hanging above the dead apple tree at the bottom of the garden.

The tree had been the last one. Not the last in the garden. The last one anywhere. There had been people once whose whole lives were spent keeping it, bringing it back from a thread of itself a hundred times over the ages, and they had run out of people before the tree ran out of dying. Now it was only wood, and she had never had the heart to take it down.

"You used to lay seven," Elias said behind her.

He was in the doorway, in the chair that carried him, a blanket over his knees though the house was warm. He didn't feel warm and cold the way other people did anymore. He felt them the way you feel news about a place you used to live. But he liked the blanket. He said it reminded him of a dog.

"There were seven of us," Yara said.

"Nine, before you were born. You wouldn't remember the good chairs." The corner of his mouth moved, which was the smile now. He rolled closer and looked at the five places, and at the fork, and he didn't say anything about the fork. That cost him. Elias liked to name things. It was most of what he had left.

The door at the end of the hall banged open. Ren had never knocked in his life, in any of his lives.

"It smells like real food," he said, coming in. "Mother. You cooked. With your actual hands." He kissed the top of her head — he could reach now; he hadn't been taller than her when he left. "You shouldn't have. I mean that as a medical fact. Most of it's just going to sit there judging me."

"You can sit at a table and you can hold a fork," Yara said. "What happens after that is your business."

"I don't really have an after-that. Not the old plumbing." He was already going round the room, touching things, the back of a chair, the lid of the pot, the way he'd touched everything as a boy to make it his. "Relax. I'll mime it beautifully. You'd be amazed how much of being a person is just knowing where to put your hands."

She didn't answer, but something in her face must have moved, because he grinned like he'd won a point.

Then Sela came in, and everyone fixed their faces before they knew they were doing it.

She'd worn one of the plain bodies for it. Yara was grateful and hated being grateful. It was a nothing colour, neat at the joints, and it walked a little too smoothly, like it had read about walking and was getting it almost right. But the face was Sela's. They always got the face right. Twenty years old, the year before she went, kept.

"You laid me a place," Sela said.

"I laid everyone a place."

"You gave me the fork." A small pause, and then, lighter: "You hid that fork behind the stove yourself, you know. I worked it out years later. You wanted one week of quiet and you were willing to let me think I'd lost it."

It was true. Yara had never told a soul. She'd half forgotten it was true until right now, and here was her daughter handing it back to her exactly, a thing only the two of them had ever known. Except a thing only the two of them had ever known was also exactly the kind of thing you could copy and keep in a box forever.

"Sit down," Yara said, to the salt. "It's getting cold."

Sela sat. The body folded into the chair and then went still. Completely still. None of the small constant shifting a person does without knowing they do it. It held that stillness a beat too long, longer than a living thing knows how to, and then Sela tucked one foot up underneath her, the way she had since she was six years old.

Nobody mentioned either thing.

They sat, the four of them and the empty places, and the candles Yara had lit because her mother lit them, and her mother's mother, back along the line to people who lit them against a real dark.

"Is he coming?" Ren asked. He meant his father. He didn't say his father. There were a few names this table had agreed, without ever agreeing, not to use.

"Tomorrow," Yara said. "For the. Tomorrow. Not for this."

"For the talking," Ren said.

"For the talking."

Tomorrow they would sit in a bigger room with people who weren't family and decide what to do about the world. Yara had kept that world her whole life, and her mother before her, and tomorrow strangers would help her decide whether to save it, and how, and what the word *save* even meant when no two of them meant the same thing by it. She'd stopped being able to hold tomorrow in her head without her hands going cold. So she thought about the soup, and passed it, and watched her family pretend.

Ren reached over and slid Sela's full glass two inches to the left, then back, just to have done something with his hands. "You're not going to drink that."

"You're not going to eat anything on that plate."

"I'm committed to the bit. You're not even trying." He looked at Yara. "She uploads herself into the cleverest machine ever built and she still sits at the table like she's waiting to be excused."

"I learned from someone," Sela said. "He left at sixteen and didn't write."

"I wrote."

"You sent a star chart. The note said, *I'm fine, the chart proves it.*"

Ren laughed, and Yara felt her own mouth go before she could stop it, and for one whole second it was just her two children being unbearable at her table, the way they always had. Then the second ended, like they all ended now. One of them had a heartbeat. One of them had a very good machine. And watching the two of them grin at each other, she honestly could not have told you which conversation she was sitting in.

Elias ate slowly, the way he did everything, like a man who'd stopped expecting to be anywhere on time. "It's good," he told her. "It tastes like." And then he stopped, and his eyes went somewhere she couldn't follow, and he left the sentence where it was. Whatever it tasted like was gone, gone longer than the word *soup* had existed, and he was the only one at the table who'd ever had it, and even he couldn't get it all the way back.

Sela was watching her mother eat. The body didn't need to aim its eyes to see, but she aimed them anyway, because that was a thing you did with a face.

"You're thinner," Sela said.

"I'm old."

"Not for one of you." A small pause. "Let me look at you properly. From the inside. I'd find things your doctors can't even reach."

"Eat your dinner," Yara said.

She heard it leave her and couldn't catch it. Sela heard it too. Everyone did. The candles burned, and nobody said the obvious thing, which was that there was nothing on that plate Sela could ever eat, and her mother had just told her to eat it anyway.

Elias noticed first.

He'd put his spoon down and was looking past her to the window, where the light came in across the table the way it did every evening at this hour. Gold, then amber, then the deep red that meant the day was done.

It was going red too fast.

Yara turned. High up, the lights that had hung so patiently over the garden all month were moving now — all of them at once, breaking apart, scattering. The light was sliding down the garden wall too fast. The shadow of the dead apple tree stretched across the grass and kept stretching. The gold went. The amber went while she watched. The red came up the way blood comes through a bandage, an hour early, a full hour, and the room filled with it.

Nobody moved.

Then Yara's hand went out across the table. It was the old reflex, the one that had reached for her children in the dark for thirty years, before they turned into things the dark couldn't get at. Her hand went toward Sela.

It stopped halfway. She still didn't know if there was anyone in there to take it.

Elias looked at the red light coming through the glass like it was a face he knew.

"I didn't think I'd live to see another one," he said.

He sounded almost glad.
