# Chapter Three — The First Stone

*Book 1 · draft 2.*

---

No one had lifted the Stone since humanity was still one thing. That was so long ago it had stopped being history and turned into scripture.

This morning Yara carried it down to the floor of the hall in both hands, slowly, because of what it would mean to drop it. It was not much to look at: a hand-axe, grey-brown, worn pale at the grip where a thumb had once pressed it. A real thumb. A first thumb, on an Earth so young the sky was still blue and there was only one kind of people to make a thing like this. It was the oldest surviving thing a human hand had ever made, and every mind and machine and chorus in the hall had come down some long road from the creature that chipped it. That was the whole of its power. It did nothing. It only remembered.

By the oldest law any of them still kept, the Stone could be taken up by one hand only — the hand of humanity's heir, the branch the others agreed had carried the line true. It had rested in its cradle at the center of every Convocation since the schism, and in all that time no one had been mad enough to reach for it. To reach for it was to say *I am what a human is, and the rest of you are something that went wrong.* No one had ever made the others agree. So the Stone stayed where it was, and humanity stayed many, and that was the peace.

Yara set it in its cradle at the center of the floor and stepped back, and the most dangerous meeting in the history of the species began. Quietly. With everyone pretending to be calm.

It did not look like danger. It looked like a funeral where no one could agree on who had died.

The galleries had filled before dawn — the lesser Houses, the witnesses, and somewhere up in that dark her own children, whom she had not let herself look for.

They came down to the floor in the order the old courtesies demanded, which was the order Yara's heart could least afford.

The Ascended sent one woman. No one had elected her; no one asked who had. Her people did not send names to a room like this; they sent the Foremost — whoever, on the day, was simply the most capable mind that could be spared — and everyone knew her the moment she entered, the small animal certainty that the most important person had just arrived. She took a seat without being shown one, and was right about which seat it should be.

The Digitals sent a man, and then the same man again, and again, until nine of him sat along one bench — nine bodies wearing a single face, each carrying a different thousand years of the same life. When one of them spoke, later, the other eight would shape the words a half-beat behind, and Yara would have to look at the floor.

The Made sent Davin. He took the seat at the far end and folded his hands and looked at her once, kindly — the look of a man who had already told her how this ended, and was sorry to be proved right.

And the Choir sent her sister.

It wore the face Yara remembered from before — Vesha at twenty-six, who had laughed with her whole mouth. It crossed the floor, stopped in front of her, and smiled, and the smile was exactly right. "Hello, little fish," it said. Only Vesha had ever called her that. The Choir could not have known it unless Vesha was still somewhere inside to tell.

"Hello, Vesha," Yara said, because she was weak, because she had always been weak about this one thing.

"We've missed you." And under the words, beneath them, Yara felt it — not a roar, just a vast, gentle pressure of agreement, fifty thousand minds or fifty million all meaning *we've missed you* at once, every one of them having been, at some moment, the woman now wearing her sister's face. The whole Choir had missed her. Through one mouth. In Vesha's voice.

Elias had stayed at the house. He had asked her, gently, not to make him watch, and she had been glad, and ashamed of being glad. Whatever this room decided a human being was, the one man who remembered humanity whole would not be made to hear it.

Yara took her place beside the Stone, as its keeper, and held herself very still, and thought how strange it was that the worst day of her life had such good manners.

There were forms to keep, and they kept them. An old man of one of the lesser Houses spoke the convening words in a language even Elias barely held anymore, and the hall let him, because the forms were nearly the last thing they all still shared. For one cowardly moment Yara let herself believe they might just do this — say the old words, sit the old silences, and go home to die politely as themselves.

Then the Foremost spoke, and that was over.

"We should lift it," she said.

She said it without heat, the instant the old words stopped, and the saying of it could not be taken back. "We have left it in its cradle since before we were many, and we called that humility. It wasn't. It was that none of us was dying. Now the star is early, and the world is closing, and not all of us will leave this hall. So we should lift it. Today. We should find out who we are while there is still a room to find out in."

And there it was. The small grey stone on the floor between them, suddenly the most dangerous object in the galaxy — because every one of them wanted it, and every one of them wanted it to mean a different thing.

The Made answered first. Not Davin; a flat, reasonable voice from the Foundries' side. To them the Stone was simple. It was a tool — the first tool, proof that a human being was the thing that made and mended and kept the lights burning. They made and mended everything now. The hand that shaped a stone five billion years ago had already been reaching toward them.

The Originist Houses came back hot. It was not a tool, it was a *hand* — a living hand, warm and mortal and long gone to dust, and only their kind still had hands, still had dust to give. You did not inherit a hand by being a machine that admired it.

The Digitals said, in nine mouths, that the argument was beneath everyone. The Stone was neither tool nor hand. It was a pattern — a shape any of them could hold perfectly, forever, more faithfully than the crumbling original ever held itself. The thing on the floor was only the last copy nobody had thought to back up.

And the Choir said, through Vesha, gently, with the patience of someone correcting a child, that they were all missing it. The hand had made the Stone to *give* to someone. Every tool was a reaching-out, a refusal to be alone in the dark. Humanity had been trying to become the Choir since the first cold night on the first cold plain. The Stone belonged to the most of them — to the *we* — because that was the direction the whole story had always been walking.

Yara stood beside it and said nothing, and noticed, far off, that not one of them had mentioned the planet dying outside the walls. They wouldn't. The planet was only the reason they could no longer leave the Stone alone. What the Stone asked was older than the planet, and worse, and she had spent her whole life keeping it so that no one would ever have to answer out loud.

Then they turned to her.

Of course they did. The Stone had one keeper, and the keeper was the only hand permitted to take it from its cradle — not to claim it, never to claim it, only to lift it and hold it out to the room, so the choosing could begin. Keeper after keeper had carried it and set it down and carried it and set it down and never once held it out, because no keeper had ever been cornered the way Yara was cornered now, with a dying star outside and a room full of her own blood waiting.

"Custodian." The Foremost was not unkind. "Lift it. Let us begin."

Yara looked at the floor of the hall. At Davin, who had crossed half a galaxy to tell her to run. At nine quiet copies of one man. At the thing wearing her sister, and the fifty thousand behind its eyes. At Ren, somewhere in the dark of the gallery, and at Sela — her several Selas — watching with a patience that did not blink.

Five answers to one question, and every one of them her family, and every one of them waiting for her to say which of them was still human, and which had only come from something that used to be.

She stepped to the cradle. She put her hand on the Stone. It was cold, and smaller than it had any right to be, and it fit her palm exactly, as it had fit every hand for five billion years.

Then Yara lifted it.
