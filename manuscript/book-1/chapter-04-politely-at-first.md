# Chapter Four — Politely, At First

*Book 1 · draft 2.*

---

The Stone weighed nothing. That was the first surprise — a law older than any of the branches, keeper after keeper after keeper, and the thing in her hand weighed less than a loaf of bread.

The hall had gone very quiet. Yara understood, distantly, that she was meant to hold it out now: lift it and offer it to the floor so the choosing could begin, so they could get on with deciding which of her children counted and which only came from something that used to. That was what a keeper did. It was the whole of a keeper's purpose — the one move every keeper in the long line had spent a life being ready for, and never once had to make.

She held it against her chest instead.

She didn't decide to. Her arms did it on their own; they had closed around children near the tops of staircases for thirty years, and they remembered. So she stood in front of the most powerful beings the universe had ever made, holding the oldest thing it had ever made against her heart, and she offered it to no one.

The Foremost tilted her head — a small, genuinely curious motion, a bird finding something it had no category for.

"Custodian," she said. "Do you understand what you've just done?"

Yara said nothing.

"The keeper lifts the Stone. The keeper offers it. The heir is chosen. Those are the steps. There is no step where the keeper lifts the Stone and keeps it." A pause, almost sorrowful. "A keeper who lifts and holds is not a keeper. She's a claimant. You have just told this room that the Originists are the heirs of humanity, and that the rest of us are the part that can be set down."

"I didn't —"

"It doesn't matter what you meant." She said it gently, and the gentleness was the frightening part — the patience of someone reading out the rules of a game whose pieces are already moving. "It matters what the law saw. Everyone in this room is bound by the law. It was the only thing keeping the room from becoming something else."

Then the sky went out.

Yara felt it before she understood it — a change in the air, the particular wrongness of a held breath let go all at once. Someone cried out. She looked up, because the hall had no roof; the Custodians had built it open to the sky, a place meant to be watched over.

She had long ago stopped seeing the lattice. It was always there; you stopped looking up. That vast, patient web of shades and mirrors hung between Earth and its swelling sun — holding the killing light down to something a garden could live under, standing between a world and a cinder. It threaded the whole sky, far too fine for its size, and it had not gone dark in her lifetime, or in any lifetime she had been told of.

Panel by panel, in a wave that crossed the heavens faster than any weather, it was going dark now.

And in the dark behind it, all the things she never let herself look at. The fleet — thousands of them, every branch of what humanity had become, hung over the cradle to watch it end. The Moon that was not the old Moon; they had taken the old one apart for the lattice an age ago, and the pale smooth thing keeping its place now was younger than the law she had just broken. And past all of it, swollen and close and the wrong colour, near enough that a patient eye could watch it move: the Sun.

Somewhere in the galleries one voice screamed, briefly, and stopped itself. Even the terror here had manners.

On the bench of the Continued, one of the nine bodies looked up; the other eight kept their eyes on the Stone in her arms, dividing the labour of fear. Beside them the thing wearing Vesha had gone absolutely still, and under the stillness Yara felt the pressure she had felt at the greeting — fifty thousand minds, or fifty million, flinching in the same instant through one borrowed body. When it spoke it spoke once, quietly, to no one in the hall.

"We remember dark," it said.

Up in the gallery every body her daughter had brought turned its face to the sky in the same moment, a row of one Sela, looking up in unison. And past the hill, alone in the house, an old man was sitting under that same sky with a blanket over his knees. Yara found her free hand pressed flat over the Stone, as if of the two of them the Stone were the one in danger.

It was the Made.

Of course it was the Made. They had built the lattice. They kept the lattice. And some mind among the Foundries had decided that a room which had just named the Originists the heirs of humanity might do with a short reminder of who, precisely, had kept the Originists' sacred world from burning for the last several billion years.

Across the floor, Davin was on his feet. He was the only one standing, and he was not looking at the sky. He was looking at her, and his face was doing something it had not done in a very long time — not anger; something worse, something like grief with the sound turned off. He hadn't ordered it. She knew it in her body, without being told. He had crossed half a galaxy to get her out before exactly this. But the Made had no single hand on the switch. They had millions, scattered across the dark, and one of them, somewhere, had heard the law name an heir and done the arithmetic and pulled a single panel's worth of mercy back to make a point.

One panel. A flicker. The lattice was already lighting again, the wave reversing, the sky stitching itself shut. It had lasted less than the time it took to understand it. Down in the garden, nothing had had the time to burn.

But every soul in the hall had just watched the sky of the homeworld blink off and on like a lamp in a room that somebody else paid for. And every one of them understood the sentence buried in it, the way you understand a knife set on the table in the middle of a meal: *this world lives because we permit it. Choose your heirs with care.*

It was not only the hall. Yara made herself think of the fleet, the thousands, and the worlds behind them — the billions she never pictured, because the number did nothing but hurt — all of them watching this now, the feeds carrying it out across the slow dark to every branch on every world: the Custodian with the Stone clutched to her chest, and the sky of Earth flickering above her head like a threat written in physics. Whatever she had meant in this room, it had been a family argument for about four seconds. Now it was history. Now it was moving. And she was holding the thing it would move around.

The Foremost watched the sky finish closing itself. Then she looked back at Yara, and there was something in her face that was almost sympathy.

"You see," she said. "Politely was always going to be the short part."

Davin had used the same word in the garden, hours ago, with the dew still on the grass. *They'll race. Politely, at first.* They had all seen it coming, then. Every one of them had walked into this hall already knowing, and they had come anyway. The only piece on this floor that nobody had a plan for was the one in her hands.
