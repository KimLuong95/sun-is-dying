# Chapter Five — The Guests

*Book 1 · draft 2.*

---

> *Whoever holds the Stone speaks for the species. This is the whole reason no one may hold it.*
> — marginal note in the keeper's records, unsigned

The most dangerous object in the galaxy spent the night on Yara's kitchen table, between the salt and a bowl of plums nobody had touched.

No one had tried to stop her taking it. That was the part she kept turning over. The session had ended the way a dropped glass ends, everyone standing very still around the pieces, and she had simply walked out of the hall with the Stone against her chest, through the garden, into her own kitchen — and the law, which had a word for keepers and a word for heirs and a word for thieves, had no word at all for a woman who picked the species up and carried it home. They had written rules for every hand that might reach for it. Nobody had written one for a mother.

"You know there are about four hundred million people watching this house right now," Ren said at breakfast. He'd pulled the feeds up somewhere behind his eyes; she could tell by the slight delay in him, like a man listening to two rooms at once. "Four hundred and six. It's the most-watched building in the galaxy. It's beating the Sun."

"Then they can watch me butter bread."

"They are. Believe me." He reached over and turned the Stone a quarter-turn where it sat, the way he'd straighten a fork, and she managed — barely — not to slap his hand. "It's smaller than it looks on the feeds. Are we just going to keep it next to the salt?"

"Until I know what I'm doing with it," Yara said, "it stays where I can see it."

The Foremost came in the morning, alone, on foot, up through the garden like a neighbour. Yara watched her stop at the dead apple tree and look at it for a long moment — not the way tourists looked at it, but the way you'd read a headstone — and then she was at the open kitchen door, asking, with complete courtesy, whether she might come in.

She sat where Davin used to sit. There was no way she could have known that.

"I won't stay long," she said. "I came to tell you what happens next, because I think you're the only person involved who genuinely doesn't know." She folded her hands. "You have a little time — weeks, maybe a season — while everyone is still deciding what you are. A keeper gone mad. A claimant. A thief. Sentiment makes them slow; nobody wants to be the one who took the Stone off the grieving Custodian. But the moment the first faction decides, the others will follow inside a day, and then it will not matter what you meant, only what you hold."

"And you came to ask for it."

"No." Her eyebrows moved; it was almost amusement. "I came to ask you to convene the choosing. Now, on your terms, while terms are still yours to set. Whoever the Stone goes to afterward — that's the room's business. Mine is that the question gets answered while there's still a room."

"And the day after it's answered," Yara said, "what is it to you? If your people win. What do you do with it?"

The Foremost looked at the Stone on the table, between the salt and the plums, and gave the honest answer at once, the way other people flinched.

"Nothing," she said. "It's a rock. The day after, it's a rock again. It's only dangerous while no one knows whose it is." She stood, and smoothed her coat, and at the door she paused. "You're hoping someone will tell you there's a way to keep everything. There isn't. I'd know — I looked."

"And if the choosing chooses wrong?"

"I'm not often wrong."

"That wasn't the question," Yara said.

The Foremost stood in the doorway a moment longer than her schedule could have liked. Then she went back down through the garden, past the dead tree, and did not look at it twice.

The Continued came at noon, three of him this time, and stood in a shallow arc in the garden because not all of them would fit comfortably in the kitchen and he was too polite to choose which of himself to leave outside. The offer was already made before the door finished opening — he was old, and oldness in his kind meant he had stopped wasting moves.

"Let us record it," all three said, nearly together, the outer two arriving a half-beat behind the middle like a sound finding the walls. "Perfectly. Every grain, every fracture, every atom in its place. We ask for nothing else — not custody, not the choosing. Once it's held, it's safe. Whatever happens to the original afterward, nothing is lost."

"You said that about my daughter," Yara said.

The middle one was silent. The outer two looked at her with an expression she had no name for — and she understood, slowly, that it was the half-beat itself: the oldest of him already grieving what the youngest of him was still saying.

"And nothing was," he said at last, all three of him, quietly. But he didn't ask again, and when they went, the three of him walked down the path single file, like one man leaving three times.

Davin came in the afternoon and did not knock, which meant something had changed, and did not mention the Stone, which meant everything had.

He went out to the garden wall — to the north corner, where the courses had gone loose, where she'd put her hand the morning he told her the years were gone — and he spent an hour taking the fallen stones up out of the grass and setting them back where they belonged. He worked slowly, by hand, although she knew his hands could have done it in a minute. It was the only sermon his people had ever had: things should be kept standing.

When he was done he stood at the kitchen door, and looked at the Stone next to the salt, and then at her, and whatever he had crossed the garden to say, he set it down somewhere inside himself and said instead:

"Your wall was failing at the north corner. It isn't now."

He was the only one of them, all day, who didn't ask for anything. She stood at the window and watched his ship go up without bending a blade of grass and thought that there ought to be a law against being loved like that, in a currency you couldn't spend.

The Choir came in the evening, and Yara did not open the door.

She stood in the hallway with her hand flat on the wood, and on the other side of it her sister's voice said, "We know you're there, little fish. Your breathing changed on the stairs."

"Go home, Vesha."

"We are home," the voice said, gently. "We brought it with us. That's the whole of what we came to say — no, don't argue, we didn't come for the Stone. We came because of the week you're having. You shouldn't be alone in it. You've been alone in it for forty years, and you never had to be, and we have never once stopped leaving the door open." A pause, and then, in exactly the voice that used to come down through the dark from the top bunk: "That's all. Eat something. You forget."

Yara stood with her hand on the door for a long time after the voice stopped. There were no footsteps. There wouldn't be. It was one body among more bodies than there were stars in the old sky, and it had simply stopped being where she was.

Of all of them, she thought, the monster was the only one who'd asked for nothing but her.

Sela had not come down since the hall. None of her.

That was its own kind of message, and Yara turned it over all through the evening meal, which was the two of them and Elias and an empty fourth chair she had laid anyway, out of spite at the universe. Ren performed his dinner. Elias ate his, slowly, with attention. At one point Ren's hand went to his opposite wrist — two fingers, light, resting there the exact way a boy checks whether a graze has stopped bleeding — and he caught her watching and turned the gesture into reaching for the bread, and smiled, and told a story about a moon he'd raced across when he was young and stupid. She let it go. The day had used up her courage, and he knew it, and that was why he'd chosen tonight to let her see.

It was Elias who heard the ships.

Not the way he heard most things now, late and from far away — he lifted his head while the sound was still only a pressure, the oldest ear in the universe catching the note it had been waiting for. He said nothing. He pushed himself up out of his chair, which he never did, and went out through the kitchen door into the garden, trailing his blanket, and Yara and Ren found him standing under the first stars with his face turned south.

The ships were coming down the southern sky in a slow line, lights strung out one after another after another, descending toward the old sea beds beyond the hills. They were not sleek. They were heavy and patient and there were a great many of them, and they did not hail the house, or the hall, or the fleet above, or anyone at all.

"Pilgrims," Ren said. He had the feeds behind his eyes again; she could see him reading. "Mother — there are a hundred thousand people on those ships. Baseline, most of them. Some Ascended. Some of everything." A pause, and the cleverness went out of his voice and left it bare. "They've filed no return plans. None of them. They're not asking for landing rights, they're — " He stopped.

"They're not asking," Yara said, "because you don't ask permission to come home."

Elias stood very still, watching the lights settle toward the dark country in the south, and when he spoke it was in his small way, from a long way off, a country he was passing through.

"When I was young," he said, "and a forest was ending — a real one, the last of something — people used to go and stand in it. Whole families. They'd pack food. You couldn't save it, and you couldn't watch it from a window, either. So you went and stood with it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "I never could decide if it was the most useless thing we did, or the most human. I used to think those were different things."

The lights kept coming down, ship after ship after ship, people arriving in their tens of thousands with no plans for leaving, come to be on Earth when the Sun went out.

Yara had spent her whole life keeping this world alive. She had broken a law older than the branches yesterday rather than hand it to anyone. And standing in her garden between her ancient son and the oldest man alive, watching the Faithful Dead come down like slow snow on the south, she understood for the first time that the thing in the sky everyone was racing to decide — save it, scan it, shell it, sing it into the union — had one more faction, quieter than all the others, and its claim was the most human and the most terrible of all.

*Let it end. And let us be in it when it does.*

Nobody had a plan for them either.
