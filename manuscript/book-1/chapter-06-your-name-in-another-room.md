# Chapter Six — Your Name in Another Room

*Book 1 · draft 1.*

---

> *Underneath everything — in the way the numbers of reality lean — there is a pattern that looks
> meant. It has been there since the beginning, and it has been measured for a hundred million
> years. The faithful call it the Maker's fingerprint. The Silence calls it a face seen in
> clouds. No one has ever read it clean.*
> — from a primer on the Pattern, author long lost

On the third night after the ships came down, someone knocked at the kitchen door.

Ren never knocked. The Choir had knocked once, and Yara had stood on the other side of the wood and let it talk to the grain. She stood in the hallway now with the lamp in her hand and knew, by the smallness of the sound, who it had to be. Sela knocked the way she had as a girl coming home late: two taps, a wait, and one more, apologising for itself.

She was alone on the step. One body, the plain colour, with the whole night behind her.

"I came as one," she said. "It seemed like the kind of conversation you have as one."

Yara stood aside and let her daughter in.

She made tea because her hands wanted a task. Sela took the cup and wrapped both hands around it, holding it for the warmth, or for the manners of warmth. The Stone sat on the table between them, next to the salt, where it had been for three days. Sela looked at it once, the way you'd glance at a sleeping dog, and didn't ask. They sat.

"The Assembly sent you," Yara said.

"No one sent me. No one knows I'm here." A pause, very small. "I didn't tell the rest of me."

She said it the way you'd say *I walked instead of taking the road.* Yara put the pot down slowly and sat with that for a moment, and decided she could not afford to understand it tonight.

"I found something," Sela said. "Four days ago. I haven't told my Assembly. I haven't told anyone. You're the first."

"Why me?"

"Because everything I bring my people gets verified." She turned the cup in her hands. "Run again. Copied. Checked against itself until it's true the way arithmetic is true. That's how we love things — we make them certain. I didn't want this made certain yet. I wanted it believed." She looked up. "You can copy a proof, Mother. You can't copy being believed."

The lamp hissed quietly. Outside, the lattice held the dark in place.

"You know what the Pattern is," Sela said.

"I know what everyone knows. There's a shape under everything. It's been there since the beginning. The faithful say it's a signature. The Silence says it's a stain on the lens. Nobody has ever read it."

"Nobody has ever read it," Sela agreed, "from anywhere we have ever stood." She set the cup down. "I've been measuring the Sun. The whole death, end to end — that's my Assembly's work, the perfect record. And underneath the noise of a star dying, there it was. The Pattern. The same smear it's always been, from everywhere, forever." She stopped. "Except it isn't. Not from here."

"What does that mean, from here."

"I don't know yet. This system. This world. Maybe this garden — I don't know what *here* means, that's half of what frightens me." She searched for the human size of it, and found it. "From everywhere else, it's noise. From here it's like hearing your name in another room. You can't prove it was your name. But you turn."

Yara's hands had gone cold around her own cup. She set it down.

"What does it mean," she said again, and heard her voice come out flat and small.

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's what I came to give you — the biggest *I don't know* since the beginning of the world, and the fact that it may only be askable from here." Sela's voice stayed level, and the levelness was costing her something; Yara could not have said how she knew that, only that she did. "Mother, listen. If I tell the Assemblies, it's out by morning. And the moment it's out, Earth stops being a shrine. It becomes an instrument. Every plan changes overnight — mine, Father's, all of them. Nobody lets an instrument die quietly." A breath she didn't need. "Nobody lets an instrument alone."

"So you brought it here."

"I needed somewhere to put it that wasn't me." She said it simply. "That's what mothers are, I'm told. The somewhere."

Yara looked at her daughter's face, twenty years old, kept. She thought of the men and the choirs and the minds in the sky, all of them circling the small grey thing on her table, and now this — the whole question of why anything exists, walked into her kitchen at night by a girl who knocked.

"Why protect it?" she asked. "Earth. Your people want it copied. You want it copied. A perfect record, and then nothing that happens to the original matters. You've said it."

"My people want nothing. People want things one at a time." Sela turned the cup again, a quarter-turn, the way her brother straightened forks. "I was born here. The fork is here. You don't believe I'm your daughter, and you set my place with the fork anyway." The pause was very long, and when she spoke again there was, for the first time, a hesitation in the levelness. "It turns out I'm not finished with the original world either."

She left before the lamp burned down. At the door she stopped, half in the dark, and didn't turn around, and Yara stood in the hallway with her hand on the frame and made herself say it.

"Sela."

She had not said the name since the funeral. It cost less than she had budgeted, and that was its own grief.

The body in the dark went still — a beat, one beat, the kind a photograph holds — and then her daughter said, "Goodnight, Mother," and walked out under the lattice, and was gone.

Yara closed the door and stood with her hand flat against the wood for a long time, the way she had the night she kept it shut.

Practice, she thought. I'm practicing.
