# Chapter Seven — Another One

*Book 1 · draft 1.*

---

> *The keeper's records run unbroken from the beginning. Except that in the deepest shelves
> three seasons are missing — cut out clean, by a keeper's blade, and never once referred to
> again.*
> — survey by an archivist of the Assemblies, who was thereafter denied the shelves

She found Elias in the garden the next morning, parked by the dead apple tree with the blanket on his knees, watching the lattice change the light the way other people watch weather.

"You said something," Yara said. "The night the dusk came early. You said you didn't think you'd live to see another one. Another what?"

For a while he didn't answer, and she thought he'd drifted. Then: "The star did this before. Lurched. Long before your mother's mother. I was younger." His eyes went somewhere she couldn't follow, the long way off, a country he was passing through. "The birds stopped. All of them, all at once. You don't know what quiet is until the engineered things go quiet. And the sky learned a new colour, and then unlearned it, and everyone told each other it was over." The corner of his mouth moved. "It's never over. It's a dying star, little one. It dies the way old men do. In instalments."

"The records don't mention a lurch."

"No," Elias agreed, mildly. "They wouldn't."

Something in the mildness made her go still. "What happened? After."

"The garden died, after." He said it the way he'd say an old recipe. "Not this garden. The one before."

The morning went on being a morning. Somewhere in the dead tree, the recorded bird was singing.

"What do you mean," Yara said, "the one before."

"They grew it back. The keepers. They're good at growing back — it's most of what keeping is. You know that. You just call it tending." He patted her hand where it had closed on the arm of his chair, and she realized she was gripping it. "Seed by seed, beast by beast, out of the vaults and the old writing. It took a long time. It was very good work. You can't tell at all."

"Elias." Her voice came out careful, the voice you use on a ledge. "The records say Earth's garden is original. Unbroken. The last natural biosphere — that's the whole — people are in the sky right now because of what this place *is*."

"Mm," said Elias.

"If it was regrown — " She had to stop and start again. "If it died and was regrown, then is it even — "

"Ah," he said, and brightened, the way he did when a thing had a saying. "There was a proverb about that. Older than I am, and that's the only old thing left." He lifted one hand, beating a small slow time. "*The water goes on; the river* — " and he stopped.

He tried it again. "*The water goes on; the river* — "

The hand came down. He looked at it.

"I've lost the end of it," he said.

"Elias — "

"I remember that the end settled it." His voice was suddenly very tired. "That was the whole use of the thing. You said it, and the matter was settled, and everyone went back to their food. I remember everyone agreeing it settled it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "I just don't remember which way."

She sat with him a long time after that, and they didn't talk, and the recorded bird sang the same eleven notes it had sung for longer than there had been anyone to teach it new ones.

"Did my mother know?" she asked at last.

Elias was quiet for so long she thought sleep had taken him.

"I told your mother once," he said. "She never asked me anything again."

That night, when the house was dark, Yara took the lamp down to the records.

The deep shelves were below the cold room, below the vaults, in the dry dark where the oldest books were kept — the ones written by hand because hands were what there had been. She had been down here exactly four times in her life, each time with her mother, each time in silence, because the deep shelves were not a place you spoke.

She found the cut seasons before midnight. Three of them, just as the old survey said: three places where the record simply stopped, and a blade had been through the binding, clean and deliberate, and the next page picked up in another season entirely with no one having died and no one having been born and the garden, in the margins of the tithe-counts, very slightly wrong.

No keeper had ever spoken of it. The cuts themselves were the speech: *here is where we will not let you look.*

She was putting the third book back when the lamp caught the bottom of the page before the cut. A line of small, steady handwriting, in the margin, where the law left room for nothing.

*Forgive us. It worked.*

Yara stood in the dry dark, under the house, under the garden, under the lattice, under the dying star, and held the lamp very still.

She knew the hand. It was the hand that had taught her own hand its letters.

Her mother had known. Her mother had known all her life and said nothing, all her life, and had written it here, at the bottom of the world, where only a keeper in trouble would ever come looking.

*Forgive us*, her mother had written, to nobody.

*It worked.*
