# Chapter Eight — Shade Is a Service

*Book 1 · draft 1.*

---

> *Shade is a service. The lattice was built to keep one schedule, and the star is no longer
> keeping it. From this date the Foundries must choose, hour by hour, what stands in the sun.
> Petitions will be heard.*
> — notice from the Foundries, posted in every camp and every House

The notice went up two days later, everywhere at once, in the flat voice the Foundries used for everything from harvest yields to the end of the world.

Davin brought it to the house himself. He came up through the garden in the early light with a flat case under his arm, and knocked at the open kitchen door, and waited to be asked in, and Yara understood from all of it — the case, the knock, the hour — that this was not a visit.

He laid the case on the table beside the Stone and opened it into a map of the world.

It was beautiful and it was terrible. Earth, whole, turning slowly in light — and over it the lattice, drawn fine as lace, and under the lattice, colours. What the shade could still hold, hour by hour, as the Sun worsened. What it could hold at a cost. And, in a red that did not blink or glow or do anything at all to soften itself, what it could no longer hold at all.

"The new schedule," Davin said. "The star is spending faster than the lattice was built to catch. Not by much, yet. By enough. Something has to stand in the sun, Yara. The Foundries are required to choose what, and they would prefer—" the smallest pause, the sound of a man translating his people into something a kitchen could hear— "they would prefer not to choose alone."

"You want the Custodian to bless it."

"They want the Custodian to share it."

"Those are the same thing wearing different coats." She looked at the red. The southern lowlands, where the old sea beds ran. Where a hundred thousand pilgrims were camped in ships that had filed no return plans. And north of it, amber: the long valleys of the garden country, the last wild places, the woods that had been grown back seed by seed by people whose names no one kept. The map said, plainly, in the Foundries' flat colours: *not both.*

"The living lands," she said, "or the living people. That's the choice you've brought to my kitchen."

"That's the choice the star brought. I'm only the case it came in." He said it without flinching, and that was Davin entire: he would carry any weight you liked, and call himself the handle.

Yara studied the map. Something was wrong with it, and it took her a moment to find what, because the wrongness was a small island of green in every projection, every hour, every worsening — one patch that never went amber, never went red, in scheduling that had been built to spare nothing.

The house. The garden. The hall. Her.

"What's this," she said, though she knew.

"Protection class," Davin said. "Locked. Unpetitionable."

"Who petitioned it?"

"No one petitions what I do," he said quietly. "I built half the lattice schedule, Yara. I'm allowed one standing instruction in it, and I spent mine a long time ago."

The kitchen was very still. The Stone sat between them on the table, between the salt and the map of everything.

"Take it off," Yara said.

"No."

It was the first time in forty years he had refused her anything to her face, and he did it the way he did everything now — gently, evenly, with no door left open behind the word. She looked at the man she had married, standing in her kitchen having pre-decided her survival like maintenance, like a wall re-set before it knew it was falling, and she could not tell, she genuinely could not tell, whether she wanted to thank him or strike him. Both feelings used the same hand.

"I asked to be the one to bring you the notice," Davin said, closing the case. "That's all I'm allowed now. Errands." He paused at the door. "File a petition about the protection class if you like. It will be heard. It will lose."

All that day the answers came in, and Ren sat at the table reading the feeds aloud, and his voice got quieter as he went.

"The Assemblies offer emergency holding," he read. "Any person in an unshaded zone may be — " he let the word sit — "*held*. Recorded. Free of charge, in perpetuity, until resettlement." He scrolled. "The Choir has opened the union to all displaced persons, no preparation required, families welcome — they actually wrote *families welcome*, mother. The Ascending offer adaptation. Heat-fast bodies inside a season. Painless. Reversible." He looked up. "It's not reversible. Nothing about us is reversible, that's the whole — " He stopped himself, and laughed wrong, and put the feed down. "They're not offering shelter. They're offering exits. Every branch in the sky is standing at the fire saying *the door's open, just take off the thing you are and leave it.*"

The petition from the camps arrived at dusk.

It was very short, and it was addressed not to the Foundries but to the Custodian, because the pilgrims were old-fashioned in the way of people who have decided to die: they went through the house. Yara read it standing, at the window, in the last of the on-time light.

*To the Keeper of Earth. We ask that the shade be withdrawn from the southern camps first, entire, and from no living land. We came to be here when it ends. We would be grateful if it could begin with us.*

It was the first petition in the history of the species to ask for the sun.

By morning it had more signatures than the Convocation had members.
