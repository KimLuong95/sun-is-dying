# Chapter Nine — The Binding

*Book 1 · draft 1.*

---

> *A keeper who only keeps will lose everything. The records do not say this, so I am writing it
> at the bottom of a page nobody reads, for whoever comes after me.*
> — the keeper's records, in the same unsigned hand

Yara carried the Stone back to the hall wrapped in a kitchen cloth.

She had thought about ceremony. There were robes for this, somewhere in the house, and litanies, and a slow proper way of walking that the records prescribed for keepers on keeping business. She took the dish-towel. Some part of her — the part her mother had written margins for — understood that the cloth was the argument: *this has been on my table for a week, and the world did not end of it. Remember that the terrible thing is small, and washable, and ours.*

She had called the session herself, which a keeper could do and none ever had. They came. They were always going to come. The hall filled in the old order — the Foremost alone, the Continued in his nine, the Choir wearing Vesha, the Houses, the Made in their grey — and the galleries filled above them, and somewhere up in that dark her children sat, and Yara walked to the center of the floor and unwrapped the Stone and held it up in one hand, in the cloth, like a woman who had been interrupted drying it.

"You all know the law," she said. "I broke it. The keeper lifts, the keeper offers, the room chooses — three steps and no fourth. I made a fourth. I'm not going to apologise for it, because every one of you has spent the week proving why I shouldn't." She let that sit. "You came to my kitchen one at a time, and not one of you asked for the Stone. You asked for the *holding* of it. Because it's written in my House's records, in a hand older than your claims: whoever holds the Stone speaks for the species."

She set it down on its cradle, and did not let go of it.

"So I'll speak.

"There will be a choosing. I will lift this Stone and offer it, the way the law says, and one of the five kinds will be named heir to all humanity, and whatever that means after, it will mean. I bind myself to that. Witness it." A breath. "And here is when. The choosing happens in the last light. On the day this world dies — at its final sunset, with Earth still under our feet — and not one hour before.

"Until then, the world is sealed. No scan. No shell. No harvest. No song. Nothing of Earth is taken, copied, rebuilt, or gathered in — not a stone, not a seed, not a dying woman in a southern camp. The kind that touches this world before its last day forfeits the choosing. Forever. Witness that too."

The hall did not erupt. She had braced for eruption; she got the other thing, the worse thing, the sound of very old minds doing arithmetic. The nine bodies of the Continued turned and looked at one another — all nine, at once, which she had never seen — like a man checking his own work in nine mirrors.

The Foremost rose. "You understand what you've done," she said, and it wasn't the question it had been in this hall a week ago. "Every plan in the sky needs time, and you've just put all the time behind a single hour. You haven't stopped the race, Custodian. You've compressed it. You've made the last day of the world the most dangerous hour in the history of the species."

"The hour was always going to be dangerous," Yara said. "Now it has a door, and the door has a keeper."

"And the keeper has a family," the Foremost said — not unkindly, which was the worst way she could have said it — and sat down.

They assented. That was the thing about the law: it was the last room all five kinds still lived in, and she had locked them in it with their own reverence. The Houses assented gladly, weeping, some of them. The Made assented by notice, flat as weather: *the schedule will hold to the final day where physics permits.* The Foremost assented "pending nothing," which Yara understood to be the closest thing to respect the Meritocracy issued in public. The Choir assented through her sister's mouth, warm as ever: "We will be there, little fish. All of us." And Davin — Davin had stood at the back through the whole of it and said nothing, and looked at her the way you look at weather you predicted and hoped against, and left before the forms were done.

Everyone agreed.

She told Elias about it that night, at the kitchen table, with the Stone back in its place by the salt — about the binding, and the sealed world, and the assent of all five kinds, given politely, almost gently, with hardly an argument.

Elias was quiet for a long time.

"Everyone agreed," he said at last, as if tasting it.

"Everyone agreed."

He looked past her, the long way off, at the country he was always passing through.

"That was true the last time, too," he said.
