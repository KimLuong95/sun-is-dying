# Chapter Ten — The Miracle

*Book 1 · draft 1.*

---

> *Stand where the light can find you. Ask for nothing. Carry nothing out. Lie down where you
> were lit.*
> — pilgrim song, heard in the southern camps

The morning after the Binding, Yara found her son in the garden at dawn, sitting on the wall Davin had mended, doing nothing at all.

It was the nothing that stopped her. Ren did not do nothing. Ren touched things, straightened things, read three feeds behind his eyes while performing breakfast with his hands. The man on the wall was just sitting, with the early light on him, slow as Elias. She brought out two cups and gave him one, and he held it and didn't make the joke — any of the jokes, and there were at least four available, she could see him not making them — and that was how she knew.

"How long have you been sick," she said.

He looked at his wrist, the opposite one, where the two fingers went when he thought no one watched.

"We don't call it sick," he said. "We call it *divergence*. Design debt. You change a thing, and the change wants changes, and you make those, and they want more, and somewhere down the line the sum of it stops agreeing with itself." He turned the cup. "We're built like arguments, Mother. Eventually the body loses."

"How long."

"Less than the Sun, probably." The laugh came out sideways and didn't hold. "We're racing. Nobody can call it. It's actually very funny if you stand far enough back, which I can't, because it's my — " He stopped, and looked at the garden, and was for one whole breath sixteen again, the boy at the window the night the light went wrong. "I check it every day. There's a number. It was supposed to scare me less, having a number. That was the design intent. I'd like a word with the designer."

"Ren — "

"Don't." He said it gently, before the offers could form. "I've seen the exits. All of them. I could cross over — and be a thing that remembers being me. I could go to the Choir and be a syllable in somebody else's sentence. Father's people would rebuild me bolt by bolt into something that doesn't lose arguments." He shook his head slowly. "They all cost the thing I'd be saving. Turns out I'm yours after all, Mother. I'd rather end as myself than continue as the receipt."

The cup steamed between his hands, and he watched it the way Elias watched soup, like a country he was passing through.

"That's why you came home," Yara said. "Not the Convocation."

"I came home because I wanted to be somewhere I was a person before I was a project." He drank, finally — actually drank, uselessly, the whole performance for no audience but her — and made a face at the taste, and for a moment they both nearly laughed, and the nearly was the closest thing to peace the week had offered.

Then Elias began ringing the bell at the kitchen door, which he had not done in years, and they went in, and the feeds were waiting.

It had happened in the night, in the southern camps. The camps kept their own slow rhythm — Yara had learned it from the petitions: the vigils, the songs, the dying. The Faithful Dead were not all young. They had come old and sick and finished on purpose, tens of thousands of them, to let the last of their lives run out on the home ground, and every night in the camps some few hundred lay down in the vigil tents and were sung across. It was, by every account, the gentlest place death had ever had.

Last night, no one died.

Not one. Not the canyon-eyed elders at the very end of baseline spans, not the sick who had refused all repair, not the woman in the easternmost camp who had been three breaths from gone at sundown with her family singing around her. The feeds had her face. At dawn she had sat up, healed of everything, weeping in a way that was wrong for a woman given back her life — weeping the way you weep for something taken, asking the tent, again and again, *who asked them. Who asked them.*

All through the camps it was the same. The dying, un-dying. Bodies that had been promised to the last sunset, quietly serviced in the dark like machines on a schedule. Whatever had moved through the vigil tents had been thorough, and skilled, and tender, and had asked no one.

"The Binding," Yara said. Her own voice sounded far away. "They've broken — "

"They haven't." Ren was reading, fast now, the slowness burned off him. "That's the thing. Read it like a law and it holds. You sealed the *world* — nothing of Earth taken, copied, gathered, rebuilt. Nobody touched the world." He looked up. "They touched the dying. People aren't the world, Mother. Whoever did this read your law very, very carefully."

No kind claimed it. The Ascending issued sympathies for *the distress in the camps*, which was a sentence that could be read four ways. The Made noted that no Foundry system had entered the southern zones, which was an alibi wearing the uniform of a fact. The Assemblies offered — gravely, of course — to *verify* the events, and the Choir sang something low across all its bodies that the feeds could not translate.

And in the camps, a hundred thousand people who had asked only to be allowed to end with their world woke into the first morning of the rest of their unwanted lives, and the petition fires burned all along the old sea beds, and the word the pilgrims were using had already reached the feeds, and the word was not *rescue*.

The word was *theft*.

Ren stood at the window a long time, looking south, two fingers resting on his wrist.

"Somebody down there got my miracle," he said. "And they're weeping."

Yara looked at the Stone on the table, bound now to the last sunset, and at the map-case corner still leaning by the door, and thought of all the things she had sealed and the one thing she hadn't, because it had never once occurred to her that it needed sealing.

She had bound the living from touching the world.

She had never thought to bind anyone from touching the dying.
