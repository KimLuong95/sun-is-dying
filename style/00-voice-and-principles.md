# Voice & First Principles

This folder is the rulebook for *how the book is written* — the difference between a great
worldbuilding doc and a great novel. Read this first; the other files are the toolkit.

## The one-sentence contract
**Big ideas, told through small human moments, in plain language that a tired person can read on
a phone and still feel in their chest.**

## Tone
- **Foundation's scale, Dune's depth, Children of Time's wonder — but the camera stays on a
  face.** When in doubt, cut from the galaxy to the kitchen table.
- Warm, sad, and clear-eyed. Not grimdark, not cozy. The universe is dying; people still make
  tea and pick fights and love each other badly.
- Earned awe, not constant awe. Save the cosmic gut-punches for when they'll land.

## Point of view
- **Close third person, one character at a time.** We sit *behind the eyes* of whoever's POV it
  is — their thoughts, their blind spots, their wrong guesses about other people.
- One POV per scene. Never head-hop mid-scene.
- POV characters (see `/characters`): Yara (primary), Ren, Sela, Davin (sparingly), Elias (rare,
  for weight). **Never** the Choir from the inside — we only ever see it from outside.

## Language rules (the "easy to follow" mandate)
1. **Plain words beat clever words.** "Started" not "commenced." "Used" not "utilized."
2. **Explain the strange the way a person would** — through what it does to someone, not through
   a manual. We never define a technology before a character bumps into it.
3. **A reader should never need a glossary.** If a made-up word can't be understood from context
   in one beat, cut it or recast it.
4. **One new idea at a time.** Don't stack three sci-fi concepts in one paragraph. Let each land.

## The feeling we're protecting
Readers should feel they're watching **humanity's last great family argument**, not reading a
textbook about the future. Every craft decision serves that. If a sentence is impressive but
cold, it loses.
