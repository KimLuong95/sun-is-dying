# Prose Craft — Making Sentences That Breathe

The anti-AI file says what *not* to do. This says what *to* do.

## 1. Specific beats general, every time
The single most powerful tool. A real detail makes a reader believe everything around it.
- Not "an old machine" → *"a console worn pale where ten thousand thumbs had pressed the same
  corner."*
- Not "she was sad" → *"she kept setting the table for five."*
- **Rule:** when a sentence feels flat or fake, it's usually too general. Zoom in until it's
  specific enough to be *only this moment, only this person.*

## 2. Show, then shut up
Give the reader the evidence and trust them with the verdict.
- Show the cold cup, the unmade bed, the unanswered message — and stop. Don't add "she was
  grieving." (See tell **D**.)
- The feeling the reader *reaches for themselves* is the one that sticks.

## 3. Concrete sensory anchors
Every scene needs a few physical things the reader can touch: temperature, light, sound, smell,
the weight of an object. Especially in sci-fi — the stranger the idea, the more we need ordinary,
bodily detail to keep it human and real.
- One good smell or texture grounds a whole alien room.

## 4. Rhythm is meaning
- Short sentences for impact, shock, decision. *He said no.*
- Long sentences for drift, memory, dread building.
- **Put the most important word last.** Sentences land on their final beat — end on the thing
  that matters, not on a limp clause.

## 5. Strong verbs, few adverbs
- Let the verb do the work: "whispered," not "said quietly." "Slammed," not "closed forcefully."
- If you need an adverb, the verb is probably weak. Swap the verb instead.

## 6. Cut filler openers
Hunt these sentence-starts and usually delete the first few words:
- "He began to walk" → "He walked." ("began to," "started to," "proceeded to" — almost always cut)
- "She felt that it was wrong" → "It was wrong." (in close POV, "she felt/thought/realized" is
  often removable — we're already in her head)
- "There was a silence" → make the silence *do* something.

## 7. Metaphor: rare, fresh, and from the character's world
- A metaphor should come from *this* character's life, not a stock bin.
  - The Made would compare grief to *a worn bearing that should have been replaced.*
  - An Originist would reach for *a fruit left too long on the branch.*
- One strong, surprising image beats five tired ones. If a metaphor could appear in any book,
  cut it.

## 8. Paragraphs: short, for the phone
- This book will often be read on a screen. Keep paragraphs lean. White space is pacing.
- A one-line paragraph is a drumbeat. Use it on purpose, not by accident.

## 9. Repetition — on purpose only
Deliberate repetition is powerful (a phrase that returns, changed, carries an arc). Accidental
repetition (the same word twice in three lines, the same sentence shape) reads as careless.
- Read aloud. Your ear catches what your eye misses.

> **Master habit:** read every finished page **out loud.** If you stumble, the sentence is wrong.
> If you get bored, the reader already left. The ear is the most honest editor.
