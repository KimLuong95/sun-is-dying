# Worldbuilding on the Page — Lore Without Lectures

You did the hard work in `/world`, `/factions`, and `/religions`. The trap now is dumping it into
the story. This file keeps the sci-fi from getting "too technical" — the thing you specifically
wanted to avoid.

## The iceberg rule
Know everything. Show almost none of it. The depth in the design docs should make the story feel
*solid* without being explained. A reader feels the 90% under the water; they don't need to see it.

## Deliver lore three ways only
1. **Through trouble.** We learn how uploading works because a mother is grieving a daughter who
   says she didn't die — not from a paragraph about mind-scanning. Lore arrives *as conflict.*
2. **Through the senses.** We learn the Sun is dying by the color of the light on the wall, the
   heat that wasn't there last year. Show the *effect* on a body, not the astrophysics.
3. **Through what people argue about.** Two characters fighting reveal the world faster and
   warmer than any narrator.

## The "no manual" rule
Never stop the story to explain a thing. If the reader can't follow a moment, fix it with:
- a character reacting in a way that implies the rule, or
- one plain clause folded into the action — *"the jump would cost them a year they didn't have"* —
  never a lecture.

## Introduce strangeness gently
- **One new concept per scene**, ideally. Let it land before adding another.
- First time we meet a strange thing, anchor it to something ordinary and human: a smell, a
  family habit, a fear we recognize.
- Name things plainly. "The Choir." "The Made." "The dying Sun." Plain names let strange ideas in
  without friction. Avoid invented jargon unless a plain word truly won't do.

## Resist the urge to be impressive
The goal isn't to show how clever the world is. It's to make the reader *forget it's invented.*
- If a passage exists to flex worldbuilding, cut it. The world should serve the family, never the
  other way round.
- A reader who's crying about Yara and Sela will believe any physics you want. A reader you've
  bored with physics won't care about anyone.

## The test
After a lore-heavy passage, ask: **could I cut the explanation and lose nothing the reader
actually needs?** Usually yes. Cut it. Let them feel smart for keeping up.
