# Epigraphs & Navigation — How the Reader Never Gets Lost

The style kit bans infodumps, names plainness a virtue, and trusts the reader — and Chapters 1–5
proved the failure mode: **all subtext, no orientation.** Beautiful sentences, lost readers. The
fix is not simpler prose. It's a navigation layer.

## The epigraph system (the Dune trick)
Every chapter opens with a short quotation from an **in-world document.** This solves the central
contradiction of the project:

> The narrator must never lecture — but a *document* may be as plain as it likes, because
> plainness is the document's voice, not the author's.

A child's primer can say "there was one world and one kind of people." A writ can list the five
branches by name. A law can spell out the three steps. The chapters keep their restraint; the
epigraphs carry the map.

### Rules
1. **Short.** 1–4 sentences. An epigraph is a signpost, not a prologue.
2. **In-world and sourced.** Every epigraph has an attribution line, and the attribution is
   itself worldbuilding (*"from a child's primer, printed on paper, kept in the Custodian
   house"* — paper tells you it's a relic).
3. **Each one delivers a KNOW-item** from the [narrative map](../story/04-narrative-map.md) —
   plainly, in words a tired phone-reader catches. If a chapter's epigraph is merely atmospheric,
   it's wasted.
4. **Plain numbers are handrails.** "Five kinds." "Three steps, and no fourth." Documents may
   count; narrators shouldn't have to.
5. **The sources roster** (extend as needed): the child's primer · keeper's law and the keeper's
   records (marginal notes, unsigned, are gold) · the convening writ and minutes of the Last
   Convocation · Foundry notices and logs · Choir liturgy · pilgrim songs of the Faithful Dead ·
   Assembly records · [the proverb bank](06-proverbs-and-sayings.md).
6. **Epigraphs may know more than the cast** — dread by document — but must never spoil a twist
   or answer a mirror-question (Rule 7).

## The orientation rules (inside the prose)
1. **First half-page test:** every chapter must answer *where are we, when is this relative to
   the last chapter, who is in the scene* within the first half-page — woven into motion, never
   as a recap paragraph.
2. **Say the load-bearing thing plainly, once.** Subtext for emotion, plain text for facts the
   plot stands on. The reader may infer that Yara is grieving; the reader must never have to
   infer what a Convocation is.
3. **Name things on the page.** If the docs have a name (the Convocation, the lattice, the
   Faithful Dead), the page uses it — names are handles, and handles are how readers carry things.
4. **After drafting:** update the [reader-knowledge ledger](../story/05-reader-knowledge-ledger.md).
   If a KNOW-item didn't land plainly, the chapter isn't done.
